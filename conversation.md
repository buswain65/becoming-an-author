## Stage 1: Premise and Theme

**The idea in plain language**

Your **premise** is your story compressed into one sentence. A useful formula is:

> *When [inciting event], a [specific character] must [goal], or else [stakes].*

Your **theme** is the question the story explores underneath the plot. It's not a moral you preach; it's something your hero's journey tests. The best themes connect directly to the hero's weakness, so the plot forces him to confront it.

**Example: *The Hobbit***

- *Premise:* When a wizard volunteers him for a quest, a comfort-loving hobbit must help a band of dwarves steal back their treasure from a dragon, or lose his chance at anything beyond his armchair (and possibly his life).
- *Theme:* Can an ordinary person find courage? Bilbo has no magic and no warrior training. He wins through luck, cleverness, and growing nerve.

That's a close cousin to your book, which is a good sign: "ordinary man without powers" is a proven theme. Your twist is that the world *expects* him to be special and punishes him for not being.

**The comedy angle**

In satire, theme does double duty: it also tells you what you're mocking. Most isekai is a power fantasy where the hero is instantly special. Your book seems to be poking at that, and possibly at the idea that people are only worth what they can be used for. Knowing your target keeps the jokes pointed in the same direction instead of scattering.

## Stage 2: Evan

**The idea in plain language**

A strong protagonist is built on a chain of five pieces:

1. **The wound:** something in his past that hurt him.
2. **The lie:** the false belief he took from it ("I'm not worth much").
3. **The want:** what he consciously chases (to go home).
4. **The need:** the truth he must learn to let go of the lie.
5. **The flaw:** how the lie makes him act, which causes him trouble.

The plot's job is to keep pressing on the lie until he can't hold onto it anymore. His **strengths**, meanwhile, are how he survives long enough for that to happen.

**Example: Sam Vimes in *Guards! Guards!* (Terry Pratchett)**

Vimes is the drunken captain of a city watch nobody respects. His wound is years of being ignored and useless; his lie is that nothing he does matters, so why try. When a dragon starts terrorizing the city, he wants to survive and keep his head down. What he needs is to believe that doing the right job well matters, even when nobody's watching. His flaw, cynicism, nearly keeps him in the bottle. By the end, he's sober and proud of his ragged little Watch. It's also very funny, which is the point: the flaw generates the jokes *and* the arc.

**The comedy angle**

In comic fantasy, your hero's way of seeing the world is a joke engine. Evan is a junior software developer, so he might treat this world like a badly designed system: magic as undocumented code, the summoners as a management team that blew the budget and is blaming the new hire. A lens like that gives you a steady supply of humor that also reveals character.

## Your exercise

2. **The lie:** In his own words, what does Evan believe about himself at the start?
3. **The need:** What truth does he need to learn by the end?
4. **The flaw:** How does the lie make him behave in ways that cause problems? (Does he give up early, avoid people, joke to deflect, refuse help?)
5. **His strengths and lens:** Besides outdoor skills, what's he good at? And how does he make sense of a crazy situation; is the "debugging the world" angle right for him, or does he have a different voice?

## Stage 3: The God and the Summoners

**The idea in plain language**

Three things make the forces around your hero work:

1. **The opposition should embody the hero's lie.** The best antagonists aren't just obstacles; they represent the false belief the hero must reject. Your summoners treat people as resources worth only what they can be used for. That's Katie's verdict turned into a national policy. Every time Evan opposes them, he's arguing with his own lie.
2. **The opposition should believe it's right.** Villains who know they're evil are flat. Villains with a logic, even a horrible one, are frightening and, in satire, funny.
3. **Allies need limits.** If the god can fix everything, Evan is unnecessary. The god's weakness is what creates Evan's job.

**Example: *Small Gods* (Terry Pratchett)**

The great god Om wakes up to discover he has only one true believer left, so he's stuck as a powerless tortoise. He needs Brutha, a simple novice, to carry him and do what he can't. The antagonist, Vorbis, is a church inquisitor who is utterly sure he's serving the god while torturing people in its name. It's hilarious and dark, and it's very close to your setup: a weak god who needs belief, a humble human doing the legwork, and an opponent whose certainty makes him monstrous.

**The comedy angle**

Satirical villains are funniest when they're competent at the wrong thing. Your summoners already think like a bad management team: they spent a fortune on a project, it failed, and they're blaming the deliverable. Lean into that logic, with budgets, approvals, and post-mortems, and they become both menacing and absurd.

## Act 3: chapters 22 to 30

**The idea in plain language**

Act 3 usually has three movements:

1. **The low point** (sometimes called the "dark night of the soul"): the hero at his worst, believing the lie completely.
2. **The rally:** something pulls him out. Crucially, it isn't luck or rescue; it's the hero choosing the truth, often helped by the people he's connected with along the way.
3. **The climax and resolution:** the hero acts on the truth, and we see the new normal.

The rally is the piece most first drafts skip, and it's what makes a climax feel earned. Without it, the hero just bounces back.

## Stage 7: Subplots and Supporting Cast

**The idea in plain language**

Supporting characters aren't decoration; each one needs a **job**. A good test is to give every character three things:

1. **A plot function:** what they do that the story needs (a skill Evan lacks, local knowledge, a way into somewhere).
2. **A thematic angle:** their own answer to your theme. In your book: how does this person see their own worth?
3. **A want of their own:** something they're chasing that isn't about Evan. That's what makes them feel like people, not helpers.

**Subplots** are the smaller stories that run alongside the main one. The best ones echo or contrast the main theme, so the middle of the book deepens instead of just filling time.

And keep the cast lean. Every named character asks readers to remember them.

**Example: the Night Watch in *Guards! Guards!***

Pratchett's Watch is a crew of misfits: a drunk captain, a cowardly sergeant, a petty thief of a corporal, and a huge, earnest young recruit. Compare that with *The Hobbit*'s thirteen dwarves, most of whom readers can't tell apart. A few vivid friends beat a crowd.

## Stage 8: Chapter Outline

**The idea in plain language**

A chapter outline turns your turning points into a sequence you can write from. You don't need every detail; each chapter just needs a one- or two-line card answering:

1. **What happens?** The main event.
2. **What changes?** Something must be different at the end: a new problem, a discovery, a decision, a relationship shift. A chapter where nothing changes is a chapter to cut.
3. **How does it end?** Ideally on a hook, a question, or a turn that makes readers keep going.

Build it in passes: first pin your turning points to chapter numbers, then fill the gaps between them, act by act.

**Example: a scene card**

> **Ch. 1 (Evan).** Dinner after the Nantahala run; instructors' stories plant the river rule; Evan almost speaks up and is talked over; he goes to sleep. *Changes:* nothing yet, by design. *Ends:* he half-wakes on something hard: "For a dream, it could be worse..."

## Stage 4: World and Magic Rules

**The idea in plain language**

Worldbuilding is where fantasy writers most often get lost: maps, languages, centuries of history, and no story. The rule for this stage is simple: **build only what the story needs, and make the rules consistent.** Everything else can wait until a scene requires it.

For magic, novelist Brandon Sanderson's well-known guidelines are a useful checklist:

1. **Magic can only solve problems as well as readers understand it.** If a power rescues the hero at the climax, readers must already know its rules, or it feels like cheating.
2. **Limits are more interesting than powers.** What magic *can't* do, or what it costs, creates the drama. (Your water mage's need to drain living things is a perfect example.)
3. **Deepen what you have before adding more.** One well-explored system beats five shallow ones.

**Example: *Mistborn* (Brandon Sanderson)**

Its magic users swallow specific metals and "burn" them for specific abilities. Steel pushes metal away; iron pulls it. Readers learn the rules early, so when the hero uses them cleverly in a fight, it feels earned, not convenient.

**The comedy angle**

Comic fantasy, like Pratchett's *Discworld*, can be absurd, but the absurdity follows its own rules. Readers laugh because the world is consistent in a ridiculous way. Your satire has an extra angle: isekai worlds often run on game-like systems (levels, status screens). Your world might deliberately *not*, which keeps disappointing Evan's expectations.

## Stage 5: The Tone Plan

**The idea in plain language**

Readers decide what kind of book they're holding within the first few pages. That's your **tonal promise**. If page one is pure horror and chapter four is slapstick, readers feel tricked. If page one hints at both, the shift feels like the story unfolding.

Three principles help:

1. **Tone is a dial, not a switch.** Even your darkest scenes can carry a trace of humor (usually in Evan's voice), and your funniest scenes can carry a trace of danger.
2. **Humor and darkness follow a curve, not a straight line.** A common shape for dark comedy: dark opening, gradual lightening as allies appear, a return to darkness at the midpoint and climax, then a warm ending. The jokes make the dark moments hit harder, and vice versa.
3. **Decide what's never a joke.** Satire punches up, at the chancellor's spreadsheets and the kingdom's euphemisms, never at the victims.

**Example: *Good Omens* (Terry Pratchett and Neil Gaiman)**

It's about the literal end of the world, much like your stakes. The humor comes almost entirely from character: a demon and an angel who've grown too fond of Earth to want it destroyed. The apocalypse never stops being real; the characters' reactions to it are what's funny.

**For your book**

Evan's deadpan voice is your most important tool. It lets you be funny during horror without mocking the horror. "I'd assumed being chosen came with a status screen. Instead, it came with a man with pliers" is a joke about *Evan's expectations*, not about the pain.

## Stage 6: Plot Structure

**The idea in plain language**

Most novels, whatever the genre, turn on a handful of key moments. You don't need every beat planned; you need these **turning points**, because everything else is just getting from one to the next:

1. **Opening:** the ordinary world and the hero's lie.
2. **Inciting incident:** the event that blows up his life.
3. **First turning point (about 25%):** the hero *chooses* to commit to the journey. There's no going back.
4. **Midpoint (50%):** a major revelation or shift. The hero stops reacting and starts acting, and the stakes rise.
5. **Low point (about 75%):** everything falls apart. The lie seems true. This is where the hero must face it.
6. **Climax:** the hero acts on the truth instead of the lie, and the main conflict is decided.
7. **Resolution:** the new normal, and what the hero chooses now that he's changed.

The key insight: **each turning point should press on the lie.** Plot structure and character arc are the same road.

**Example: *The Hobbit***

Opening: Bilbo at home, loving comfort. Inciting incident: dwarves invade his house. First turning point: he runs out the door without his handkerchief. Midpoint: in Mirkwood, he rescues the dwarves from spiders and becomes the one they rely on. Low point: Thorin's greed turns the quest ugly, and Bilbo betrays him (giving up the Arkenstone) to prevent a war. Climax: the Battle of Five Armies. Resolution: home again, but changed, and not the respectable hobbit he was.

## Stage 9: The Story Bible

**The idea in plain language**

Your plan doc is the record of *how* you built the story. A story bible is different: it's the quick reference you keep open while drafting, so you can check a name, a rule, or a planted detail in seconds instead of searching through pages of decisions.

A good one is short and organized for lookup:

1. **The core:** working title, premise, and theme in a few lines
2. **Characters:** one compact entry each: role, what they want, how they see their worth, and a voice note
3. **Hard rules:** the world's rules you must never break (readers *will* notice)
4. **Places and names:** everything with a name, including placeholders still to be named
5. **Timeline:** the history in order, so dates and counts stay consistent
6. **Plants and payoffs:** a checklist of every setup and where it pays off, so nothing gets forgotten in drafting
7. **Tone rules:** what's never a joke, and where the humor comes from

**Example:** many authors keep a "plants and payoffs" list alone. Rowling famously tracked which clues went in which book; in your case, things like the river rule, the silver inlay, the Sweden trip, and the motto all need their setups in place before their payoffs land.
