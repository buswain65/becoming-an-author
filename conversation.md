> **The idea in plain language**
> 
> A strong protagonist is built on a chain of five pieces:
> 
> 1. **The wound:** something in his past that hurt him.

>

> ## Stage 3: The God and the Summoners
> 
> **The idea in plain language**
> 
> Three things make the forces around your hero work:
> 
> 1. **The opposition should embody the hero's lie.** The best antagonists aren't just obstacles; they represent the false belief the hero must reject. Your summoners treat people as resources worth only what they can be used for. That's Katie's verdict turned into a national policy. Every time Evan opposes them, he's arguing with his own lie.
> 2. **The opposition should believe it's right.** Villains who know they're evil are flat. Villains with a logic, even a horrible one, are frightening and, in satire, funny.
> 3. **Allies need limits.** If the god can fix everything, Evan is unnecessary. The god's weakness is what creates Evan's job.
> 
> **Example: *Small Gods* (Terry Pratchett)**
> 
> The great god Om wakes up to discover he has only one true believer left, so he's stuck as a powerless tortoise. He needs Brutha, a simple novice, to carry him and do what he can't. It's hilarious and dark, and it's very close to your setup: a weak god who needs belief, a humble human doing the legwork, and an opponent whose certainty makes him monstrous.
> 
> **The comedy angle**
> 
> Satirical villains are funniest when they're competent at the wrong thing. Your summoners already think like a bad management team: they spent a fortune on a project, it failed, and they're blaming the deliverable. Lean into that logic, with budgets, approvals, and post-mortems, and they become both menacing and absurd.

> ## Stage 4: World and Magic Rules
> 
> **The idea in plain language**
> 
> Worldbuilding is where fantasy writers most often get lost: maps, languages, centuries of history, and no story. The rule for this stage is simple: **build only what the story needs, and make the rules consistent.** Everything else can wait until a scene requires it.
> 
> For magic, novelist Brandon Sanderson's well-known guidelines are a useful checklist:
> 
> 1. **Magic can only solve problems as well as readers understand it.** If a power rescues the hero at the climax, readers must already know its rules, or it feels like cheating.
> 2. **Limits are more interesting than powers.** What magic *can't* do, or what it costs, creates the drama. (Your water mage's need to drain living things is a perfect example.)
> 3. **Deepen what you have before adding more.** One well-explored system beats five shallow ones.
> 
> **Example: *Mistborn* (Brandon Sanderson)**
> 
> Its magic users swallow specific metals and "burn" them for specific abilities. Steel pushes metal away; iron pulls it. Readers learn the rules early, so when the hero uses them cleverly in a fight, it feels earned, not convenient.
> 
> **The comedy angle**
> 
> Comic fantasy, like Pratchett's *Discworld*, can be absurd, but the absurdity follows its own rules. Readers laugh because the world is consistent in a ridiculous way. Your satire has an extra angle: isekai worlds often run on game-like systems (levels, status screens). Your world might deliberately *not*, which keeps disappointing Evan's expectations.
